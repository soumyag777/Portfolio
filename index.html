Let's get it done

What drew me to Home Depot was how everything felt possible there. "Let's get it done" is their motto, and everyone I worked with matched it. That energy pushed me well outside my comfort zone and taught me how client relationships really work: how to pitch, how to hear "no" a gazillion times before my first "yes," how to be patient, and above all how to trust the process. On the ground, that meant going into people's homes, asking the right questions, and matching what they needed to the right product, before I had a name for that skill. Then something I witnessed at work made the next step clear: I wanted to work where business, psychology, and technology meet, not stay in just one of them.

Plain words, high stakes

I learned the discretion rules cold: why a transaction near $10k gets watched closely, why a trust account holder's information can't casually pass to their own spouse, why accuracy and security aren't optional in financial interfaces, they're the whole job. I also watched customers stumble over an internal system thick with jargon, confusion that later, thankfully, got fixed in an update.

Readable English: Student Portal Redesign

Readable English · UX Designer & Researcher · Team of 4 · Aug to Oct 2024

Readable English helps struggling readers in grades 2-12 catch up through phonics and visual guides, but its own student portal worked against that mission: young learners found it hard to navigate and unengaging, and dropped off before finishing lessons.

The redesigned portal

Hi-fi: the final student portal. The gamified island map turns lessons into a path kids move along, with the wordometer and badges alongside.

From wireframe to island map, tested at every step

Every fidelity level was tested with 5 kids across school and home settings, 15 testers total, before moving forward.

Low-fi: the start button read as the start of a lesson instead of the tutorial, so testers skipped onboarding entirely.

Mid-fi: the start button was still often overlooked, so onboarding needed a stronger visual anchor, not just clearer copy.

Hi-fi: the island map and wordometer landed well. Remaining feedback centered on separating "start a lesson" from "start the tutorial" even more clearly.

Research

Grounded the redesign in the actual people around the product before touching a screen:

  • 5 discovery interviews across the CEO, a school psychologist, and three teachers (3rd grade, kindergarten, language)
  • A 39-response survey on what keeps students engaged in a learning portal
  • An affinity diagram and empathy map synthesizing interview and survey feedback
  • A heuristic evaluation plus a WCAG color-accessibility audit, which surfaced gaps against both AA and AAA standards
  • Competitor analysis against similar learning products

That research converged on a persona, Josh, an 11-year-old 5th grader who wants to read like his classmates but gets bored and gives up on lessons, and a hypothesis: simplifying the layout, clarifying navigation, and adding gamified elements would help him start and finish lessons on his own.

Outcome

Implemented: the client put the redesign into use. Along the way I shipped a coded, interactive prototype (built in CodePen, with a custom animated loading screen to keep kids engaged through load times) and handed the client a portal built around gamified progress, an island-by-island lesson map, and onboarding clarified across three rounds of real kid testing, not just internal review.

Dance on the Edge: Vendor Experience Redesign

Non-profit website redesign · UX Designer · Team of 4 · Jun to Aug 2024 · My focus: style guide, color system, and low-fi through mid-fi mobile wireframes

Dance on the Edge is a Sacramento non-profit building community through free, family-friendly dance events and vendor markets, partnered with the city to spotlight local venues. But local vendors kept passing on their events, not because they weren't interested, but because the website gave them no clear way to apply.

Low-fi: paper sketches for the mobile homepage, laying out the event calendar and a clear path to socials and vendor info before any visual design.

Mid-fi: the events calendar, now with "Vendors" added directly to the main navigation instead of being buried.

Mid-fi: the dedicated Vendor Apply form the whole redesign was built around, vendor name, type, socials, and business info in one short flow.

Style system: button states, iconography, and a typography scale built for a playful, clean, family-friendly tone, then checked for color contrast.

Research

  • Interviewed vendors directly about their application experience: "If a website is unclear on where I can apply as a vendor, I move on to another event with a better application process"
  • Built an affinity diagram from those interviews: vendors are largely tech-savvy, prefer applying directly on an event's website over a Google Form, and choose events based on expected profit and popularity
  • Defined the target user as Cadence, a small business owner and marketing professional who was having trouble finding vendor opportunities worth her time
  • Ran a feature prioritization matrix that pointed to one clear move: a simple, dedicated vendor intake form on the site itself

That converged on a hypothesis: adding a dedicated vendor page with a homepage CTA would attract new and unique vendors to collaborate with.

Outcome

Shipped: the vendor application flow the research pointed to was fully implemented on the live site, directly increasing vendor participation.

Arcana Quest: a tarot learning game

Personal project · Built with Claude · Oct 2026

A game for learning tarot, made for spooky season and to get hands-on with new tools. I built the site with Claude. You learn 78 cards by playing, and every answer teaches you something.

The Journey: eleven realms, each with a guardian card. Beat it to unlock the next.

A question and its lesson: after every answer, right or wrong, the game explains the card so the player learns from it.

How it plays

  • Journey: 11 realms with a guardian each. Right answers strike the guardian, wrong answers cost a heart, and clearing a realm unlocks the next.
  • Arena: an endless mode with three hearts and combos, drawing from all 78 cards.
  • Grimoire: a collection that fills in as you answer cards correctly.
  • Variety: 11 question types, from naming a card to reading it upright or reversed, plus elements, zodiac signs, timing, and two-card pairings.
  • Practice that circles back: cards you have not mastered come up more often.

Gaming Conference: designing for the crew behind the event Service design exploration · Team practice project · Aug to Nov 2025 What would a conference feel like if it were designed for the people running it, not only the people attending?

A conceptual practice project. It was not built for a real conference or tested with attendees, so what follows is what I learned from research and ideas worth testing, not measured results.

The question

A gaming conference brings together players, developers, and industry leaders, and it depends on a crew of staff and volunteers that most attendees never see. I wanted to explore designing for that crew, not only for the visible touchpoints like signage, schedules, and stages.

Research

I volunteered at the Grand Prix of Long Beach to watch how crew members shape the attendee experience up close. I could not shadow a gaming conference for this project, so I used the race as a stand-in: it is a large public event run by staff and volunteers, with crowds, schedules, and many moving parts to coordinate, the same kind of ecosystem a gaming conference relies on.

  • Observed backstage operations in real time
  • Interviewed staff and crew
  • Mapped the ecosystem from crew check-in to real-time coordination to post-event debrief, using service design principles

What I learned

At the race, the clearer the crew's roles, information, and communication, the more effortless the event felt to attendees, and the reverse was also true. That shifted how I start community and service projects: I map the people behind the scenes first.

The concept I sketched

  • Crew onboarding: clear role definitions, schedules, and communication channels
  • Real-time coordination: simple tools for staff to share updates and solve problems quickly
  • Post-event feedback: a closed loop capturing insights from both crew and attendees

Tools: Field Research, Service Design, Journey Mapping, Stakeholder Interviews

CyberCar Rental: one booking flow, two devices Conceptual exploration · Product Designer · Jan to Mar 2025 · Mobile + tablet Could a booking flow feel like the same futuristic-but-warm brand on a phone and a tablet?

A conceptual design project. I have not tested it with users, so I am describing what I explored and learned, not results.

The question

Booking a rental car is a long, multi-screen flow, and a brand can easily get lost along the way. I wanted to see whether a futuristic but warm look could carry through every step, on both mobile and tablet.

What I explored

End-to-end user flows for both devices, with the handoff between them in mind from the start. I focused on emotional consistency: the brand feeling had to hold through every interaction of the flow, not just on marketing pages.

What I learned

Designing for two devices at once meant deciding early what stays identical and what adapts. It also showed me how much of a brand's feeling lives in functional screens like forms and confirmations.

Tools: Figma, Framer, Miro, iOS/Android Design Guidelines

QuantaQuid: modernizing financial services Lead Product Designer · Apr to May 2025 · Confidential FinTech client A landing-page redesign built to close a trust gap for two very different audiences.

A confidential FinTech client was struggling to convert site traffic into active business accounts, undercut by a trust gap, feature-first copy, and a one-size-fits-all message for two very different audiences.

Good morning, Jordan
$12,480.32
Payroll deposit
Today
+$3,200.00
Vendor payment
Yesterday
−$540.00
Wire transfer
Mar 12
−$1,120.00
Send Instant Payment

Concept mockup illustrating a mobile companion view for the platform (the shipped engagement was the web experience).

Approach

Treated the landing page as a credibility engine, structured around three pillars: establish authority instantly, translate features into business outcomes, and give each audience its own path through the page.

  • Hero metrics ("$2.5B+ Processed," "99.9% Uptime") placed directly below the headline, answering trust questions before friction builds
  • Rewrote technical feature names in benefit terms: "Payment Gateway" became "Instant Payments"
  • Designed distinct "For Startups" and "For Enterprises" paths so each segment sees itself in the product
  • Repeated social proof and metrics near the primary CTA to reduce anxiety at the conversion moment

What changed in the design

Conversion clarityDual-audience segmentation replaced one-size-fits-all messaging with distinct startup and enterprise journeys
Trust signalsKey financial metrics surfaced above the fold, addressing the credibility gap directly
ComprehensionBenefit-oriented copy cut technical jargon, shortening time-to-understanding for non-technical founders
HandoffAnnotated specs and WCAG-compliant components shipped with zero revision cycles
